# Global Agent Memory Frontier Map — 2026-08-24

Status: external-only frontier mapping, deliberately written **before** re-reading AAMR's own roadmap docs, so the mechanism families below reflect what the field is actually doing rather than AAMR's existing framing. The comparison against AAMR's roadmap lives in the companion document, `CROSS_PAPER_HYPOTHESIS_MAP_2026-08-24.md`.

Scope: 9 parallel research passes, roughly 150 distinct papers/systems checked, the large majority against primary sources (arXiv abstract/HTML, official repos) rather than search snippets. This is not a novelty search — nothing here claims AAMR is first at anything, and nothing here has been screened for "does this help AAMR's positioning." The brief was the opposite: map the field, including everything that could be a Frankenstein part, including things that argue against AAMR's current assumptions.

Labels: **MEASURED** (a paper's own reported number, checked against the primary source), **PAPER CLAIM** (the source's own claim, abstract-level, not independently reproduced), **CODE VERIFIED** (a repository was actually opened and its contents confirmed, not just linked), **INFERENCE** (this document's own reasoning), **HYPOTHESIS** (untested), **UNVERIFIED** (found only in a secondary source or an abstract that itself didn't give the number).

## StateMemBench / StateMem — the triggering paper, deep-verified

"Can Agent Memory Systems Track Evolving State?" — Xinyi Fan, Miri Liu, Ruozhen Yang, Siru Ouyang, Jiawei Han. arXiv:2608.19652, submitted **August 20, 2026** (4 days before this report). Preprint only, no venue indicated. **"StateMemBench"** is the benchmark (234 multi-session scenarios), **"StateMem"** is the full method, **"StateMemWrapper"/"SMW"** is the read-only wrapper applied across 6 backends — three distinct things, worth keeping separate when citing.

**Every headline number in the original brief checked out — with one important correction.** The "+32 to +67 points" (wrapper's total gain across 6 backends) and the "+15 to +32 points" (the length-and-cost-matched control's attribution to state structure) are **not two independent facts** — the second is a **nested subset of the first**. Of the total wrapper gain on any given backend, 15–32 of those points are attributable to structure specifically; the rest is attributable to other factors the control doesn't isolate. Both ranges were independently recomputed from the paper's own Table 6 raw cells and matched the abstract's claims almost exactly (32–67 recomputed; 15.0–31.7 recomputed against a claimed 15–32) — MEASURED, not just quoted.

The 44.4% "state drift" figure is real but narrower than a bare percentage suggests: 16 of 36 confirmed errors on one backbone (DeepSeek-V4-Flash), original LongMemEval (not V2), **Wilson 95% CI [30%, 60%]** — a real effect with real uncertainty, not a precise field-wide constant.

**Two distinct mechanisms, one transplantable:**
- **StateMem (full method)** has its own write path: a TurnEncoder makes one LLM call per conversational turn; a StateStore flags supersessions; a deterministic (LLM-free) Rechecker traverses a dependency graph marking affected units for recheck; one further LLM call resolves the assembled state at answer time.
- **StateMemWrapper (the thing applied to 6 backends)** is purely read/inference-time: one call that replaces the answer-generation call a backend would make anyway ("adds no LLM calls"), doing Trace (a ≤250-word chronological value-chain per relevant slot) then Resolve (four fixed precedence rules: later supersedes earlier; standing rules outrank one-off instances; derived quantities are recomputed, never reused stale; facts retire only via explicit supersession or expiry). **This is the transplantable unit** — no backend storage change required.

**A real caveat on "host-agnostic," not in the paper's own framing (INFERENCE):** the wrapper's inputs include the raw transcript directly, alongside each backend's retrieved chunks — so part of its cross-backend success may reflect having a privileged fallback to ground-truth text, not pure backend-blind robustness. Also, of the "6 backends," only 4 (Mem0, A-Mem, LightMem, MemoryOS) are genuine memory systems; 2 (BM25, dense embedding) are classical IR baselines with no memory/consolidation layer at all.

**Negative/bounding results the paper reports on itself (exactly what a transplant candidate needs disclosed):**
- Its own dependency-propagation component over-propagates on one test subset (−12.5pp); removing it leaves one backbone *better* off (0.373 vs 0.363).
- A weak backbone (Qwen-3.5-9B) neutralizes the entire memory layer — drift rate sits at 61–66% for long-context, Mem0, BM25, and StateMem alike.
- A naive question-blind 250-word summary **actively hurts**, falling below the no-wrapper baseline with the highest drift rate of any arm tested — compression that isn't question-conditioned can be worse than doing nothing.
- Drift is not universal even within the paper's own generalization check: leads on one MemoryArena subset (63.5%) but falls to 19.0% on another, where comprehension failure dominates instead (46.5%).
- StateMem "remains competitive with" — does not clearly beat — long-context baselines.

Code: not found (paper says it will be released; 4 days old, may simply not be indexed yet).

**Directly related cluster, all primary-verified:**
- **STALE** (arXiv:2605.06527, May 7 2026) — the same paper referenced elsewhere as "STALE/CUPMem"; StateMemBench cites it explicitly as concurrent work with a stated mechanistic distinction: STALE uses an LLM adjudicator over a predefined schema, StateMem uses a deterministic graph traversal. Best model 55.2% on 400 expert-validated conflict scenarios.
- **StateAuditor**, inside "When Memory Updates but Behavior Does Not" (arXiv:2608.01619, Aug 3 2026, 17 days before StateMemBench, not cited by it) — audits in the *opposite direction* (stored-state → draft, not pre-answer trace/resolve); +5.0pp on its own benchmark but explicitly "no gain" on a second benchmark (HorizonBench), matched control +0.6, not significant. A close relative in problem framing, a different mechanism, and an honest self-reported generalization failure.
- **LongMemEval-V2** (arXiv:2605.12493, May 12 2026) — code available (rare in this space), oriented at web-agent dynamic state as one of five abilities rather than conversational fact-supersession specifically; cited by StateMemBench, but StateMemBench's own 44.4% figure is on the *original* LongMemEval.
- **MemoryArena** (arXiv:2602.16313, Feb 18 2026) — shares an author (Siru Ouyang) with StateMemBench, reused directly as its generalization check.

## Negative results catalog (curated, primary-source verified)

Ranked by evidentiary strength × consequence, not by family:

1. **Deterministic freshness beats LLM-mediated conflict resolution by ~20 points** on MemoryAgentBench FactConsolidation (gpt-4o-mini 78.0%, gpt-4o 94.8%, vs. Mem0 18%, Zep/Graphiti 7% for the LLM-mediated approach) — arXiv:2606.01435, code verified, independently reconfirmed by **four separate research passes in this sweep**, the single most-triangulated finding in this whole report. Important caveat, self-disclosed by the authors between versions: a broader LongMemEval check found "no significant overall advantage" (26/45 vs 29/45) — the huge win is specific to explicit-serial FactConsolidation-style tasks, not general temporal QA. Title also appears to have changed between arXiv versions (v1 "Don't Ask the LLM to Track Freshness," a later version "Reliable Post-Retrieval Assembly for Agent Memory") — flagged, not fully confirmed by version diff.
2. **Mem0 scores 25.50% correct / 74.02% omission at the memory-*updating* stage specifically**, vs. MemOS 62.11%/37.48%, on HaluMem (arXiv:2511.03506, ~15,000 memory points, ~3,500 questions). Independently converges with AAMR's own already-logged Mem0 date/temporal defect.
3. **"Tenure crossover"**: a budgeted curated-map memory system leads at 3 weeks (96% recall of its own content) but loses to a provenance-typed graph by 9 weeks (72% vs. 90%, p=0.031) — arXiv:2607.21962. A genuine ranking *inversion* driven purely by evaluation time horizon; directly cautionary for any short-window evaluation methodology, AAMR's own included.
4. **All 18 self-inspection comparisons lose to repeated sampling at equal token cost**, 3.6–10.1 points below at 7B (larger at 1.5B) — arXiv:2607.28576, compute-matched, code released. "Reflexion, implemented as its authors describe it, never once triggered its own retry on the smallest model."
5. **1.2% corpus poisoning (plainly-worded, no jailbreak) drops accuracy 0.850→0.300; content screening catches 0 of 360 poisoned memories; provenance weighting is statistically indistinguishable from no defense** — arXiv:2608.21230, 3 days old at time of writing.
6. **Reflective self-diagnosis produces confabulated beliefs that persist across environment resets**: 0 of 121 reflections in 16 frozen environments mention the correct target object; programmatic signal extraction instead of open-ended reflection raises this from 0%→86% — arXiv:2605.29463.
7. **Raw/verbatim trajectory reuse gives *negative* forward transfer** in procedural/experience-reuse settings (−9.5%, −7.5%), concentrated on hard cases specifically (−26.1, −12.7 on hard subsets) while abstracted/insight representation gives *positive* transfer (+6.5%, +9.0%) on the same tasks — arXiv:2604.27003. Notable tension with the QA-memory literature's "raw beats extraction" finding (item 9 in AAMR's own related-work doc) — different memory type, not necessarily the same law.
8. **Cosine similarity's AUROC for separating contradicting facts from paraphrased duplicates is 0.59 — near chance** — contradicting facts are often *more* embedding-similar than duplicates, so plain dense RAG serves a superseded value 15–40% of the time across four evolving-knowledge scenario families — arXiv:2606.26511.
9. **GraphRAG underperforms plain dense RAG by 10–15 points** across NQ/HotpotQA/MultiHop-RAG/SQuALITY, traced to incomplete KG construction and lossy community summaries — arXiv:2502.11371 (general RAG literature, directly transfers to graph-based agent memory).
10. **Curated forgetting beats keep-all**: accuracy 0.294→0.420 on a poisoned/stale-corrupted subset, prompt-injection attack success 0.75→0.00, tested on a real Jetson robot-arm testbed — arXiv:2606.25115. The cleanest "forgetting helps" case found anywhere in this sweep.

**Two explicit, honestly-reported absences, not papered over:**
- **No primary-source paper found showing a forgetting/deletion *mechanism* itself deleting something later needed and causing measured harm.** Every paper touching this raises it only as introductory motivating rhetoric before pivoting to its own positive result. "Forgetting-that-didn't-happen hurts" (PersistBench: 53% cross-domain leakage, 97% sycophancy failure across 18 LLMs) is well-evidenced; the opposite direction is not. Treat any future claim of "our forgetting mechanism caused a regression" as needing its own dedicated measurement — there is no existing primary-source template for it.
- **No paper found stating hierarchical organization adds overhead without benefit**, despite a dedicated search. Either the effect is genuinely robust in tested regimes, or negative hierarchy results aren't being published/indexed at the same rate as positive ones (publication bias is a live possibility, not dismissed).

## Recency highlights

**Last 7 days (Aug 17–24, 2026)** produced roughly 20 relevant submissions — more than any other window, consistent with a fast-moving sub-field. Most significant: StateMemBench itself (above); "Utility Under Attack" (memory poisoning, item 5 above); "Remember, Verify, or Ask?" (arXiv:2608.19564 — a new evaluation axis: should information be persisted, used in-context only, re-verified, or clarified with the user; models default to verifying over asking); DreamBench-SWE (arXiv:2608.20664 — a striking small gap between deterministic verbatim-log memory (45.56%) and a hosted Mem0 configuration (53.89%) for software agents, worth AAMR's direct attention given the parallel to its own Mem0-vs-simple-baseline findings); "Beyond Memory Majority"/CAMA (arXiv:2608.19701 — names "Memory Correlation Bias," where memories from different agents sharing an upstream source get double-counted as independent evidence in multi-agent arbitration).

**Last 30 days** (Jul 25–Aug 16): PoEM (arXiv:2608.16032 — breaks a published memory-forgery defense via simple LLM rewording, finds a "capability paradox" where *stronger* models are more exploitable, 98–100% attack success on GPT-4o vs. 44% on Llama-3.1-8B); CrystalMem (arXiv:2608.00303 — "memory hysteresis," capability degradation persisting even after a cut quota is restored, since deletion/compression is irreversible without an explicit recrystallization path); "Memory Reward Inflation in Self-Improving LLM Agents" (date-flagged as possibly misattributed by the extraction tool, content independently plausible — self-graded memory agents preferentially reuse their own confidently-wrong episodes).

**Last 90 days**: "World-Model Collapse as a Phase Transition" (arXiv:2606.31399 — drift framed as a discrete phase transition with a solved plateau, narrow transition band, and collapse floor, rather than gradual decay, and "world-state fidelity fails before action validity"); "When Retrieval Fails Before It Begins"/DSGC (eviction under budget pressure discarding weakly-query-aligned context *before* retrieval ever runs, raising full-chain retention from 0.03→0.90); MemFail (a benchmark decomposing failure attribution by pipeline stage — summarization/storage/retrieval — specifically because aggregate accuracy can't localize which stage failed).

**Conference signal**: NeurIPS 2026 workshop calls (announced Aug 10, within the 30-day window) include PALM (Personalized, Aligned, Long-Term Memory for AI Systems), explicitly scoped to most of the themes in this report — a call-for-papers, not an accepted-papers list, since NeurIPS 2026 is in December.

## Did we miss something big? (assessed against everything above)

**A. Has a paper in the last 30 days fundamentally changed how to think about agent memory? — Borderline yes, one candidate.** "When Memory Becomes Authority" (item 3 in the top-10 table) is the strongest candidate: a near-universal failure mode (48/49 configurations) in a place — the consolidation boundary — that most of the field treats as solved or at least safe. It doesn't overturn an architecture, but it identifies a failure class broad enough that any future consolidation mechanism, AAMR's own included, should be checked against it before being trusted. "Useful Memories Become Faulty" is a close second, and its explicit invocation of Complementary Learning Systems theory is a genuine reframing, not just a benchmark result.

**B. Has anything appeared with stronger evidence than AAMR's current strongest candidates? — Yes, on two specific points, no on the overall direction.** The authority-collapse finding (48/49 configs) and the deterministic-freshness finding (independently triangulated four times, item 1 above) are both better-replicated and larger in magnitude than any single AAMR-internal result to date, simply because AAMR's own arena samples are still small (n≈10). Neither displaces AAMR's core hypothesis; both are stronger *pieces of evidence for specific sub-mechanisms* than anything AAMR has measured itself yet.

**C. Has anyone practically executed the "Frankenstein" strategy — compare independent systems, transplant, ablate interaction, route? — No.** Nothing in this pass does more of that pipeline than what the prior red-team search already found (AutoMEM's isolated-but-not-combined transplant deltas remains the closest single precedent). The multi-agent concurrency cluster and the graph-poisoning cluster are new *territory*, not new *evidence* on this specific methodological question.

**D. Should anything here change AAMR's next three experiments? — Two candidates worth a decision, not an automatic change.** (1) The authority-collapse finding is cheap to pressure-test against AAMR's own already-built bitemporal/provenance schema — see the companion hypothesis document for a concrete, near-zero-cost version of this test. (2) The retrieval-utilization / "zero answer dispersion" finding suggests a cheap, immediate diagnostic AAMR could run on its own already-known Mem0 failures (temperature-sampled dispersion check) before designing anything new. Neither requires abandoning the currently-planned experiments; both are candidates for a cheap fourth or fifth test, not a replacement for the first three.
