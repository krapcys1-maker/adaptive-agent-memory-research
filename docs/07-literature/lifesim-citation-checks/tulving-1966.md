## What the source says about the claim, with a locator (page, section, table or figure)

- Participants learned categorized word lists on a single trial; list length was 12, 24 or 48 words and there were 1, 2 or 4 words per category. Immediate recall was tested with or without category names as retrieval cues (abstract, p. 381).
- Cued recall exceeded noncued recall, and the advantage varied directly with list length and inversely with the number of items per category (abstract, p. 381).
- The authors interpreted this as intact traces being available in storage but not accessible for retrieval, and their component analysis suggested two separate retrieval processes: access to categories and access to items within a recalled category (abstract, p. 381; pp. 389-390 per the repository note).
- The paper explicitly cautions that recognition-versus-recall comparisons are only "partly relevant" to availability versus accessibility and that other methods are needed (introduction, pp. 381-382). Its manipulation was cued versus free recall, not recognition versus recall.

## Verdict: partly supported

The availability/accessibility distinction is supported and fits the cue-sparsity use at line 1017. The IN3 use at line 816 is not supported as written: Tulving and Pearlstone did not measure a recognition-minus-recall gap, and they warned that recognition-versus-recall comparisons are only partly relevant to the storage/retrieval distinction.

## Scope limits the digest does not carry (population, method, delay, effect size)

- More than 900 high-school students, one-trial learning of categorized word lists, immediate recall; no retention delay and no recognition condition.
- The manipulation changes retrieval cues at test (category names present or absent); it demonstrates recoverability under an added cue, not permanent storage or a physical trace.
- Recognition and cued recall change the task and should not be treated as pure measures of storage.
- The size of the cue advantage varied with list length and category size; the paper does not supply one general effect size.

At the cue-sparsity dial, keep the citation but name the paradigm: "Availability versus accessibility [B Tulving and Pearlstone 1966, cued-versus-free-recall paradigm]".
