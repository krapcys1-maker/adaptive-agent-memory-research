## What the source says about the claim, with a locator (page, section, table or figure)

- Experiment I: 45 students viewed seven films of traffic accidents and answered questions. The critical question about vehicle speed used one of five verbs. "Smashed" elicited higher speed estimates than "collided", "bumped", "contacted" or "hit" (abstract, p. 585; Experiment I, pp. 585-587).
- Experiment II: 150 students viewed one film and answered a questionnaire. One week later, without viewing the film again, they answered a set of questions including "Did you see any broken glass?" There was no broken glass in the film (Experiment II method, p. 587).
- Experiment II result: the probability of saying "yes" to broken glass was .32 in the "smashed" condition and .14 in the "hit" condition (Table 2 and text, p. 588). The authors interpret this as consistent with post-event information being integrated into the memory representation, while also considering a response-bias interpretation for the speed estimates (discussion, pp. 587-589).

## Verdict: partly supported

The paper supports the broad misinformation analogue: post-event wording can bias later reports, including a detail that was never present. The digest's use is narrower than the paper's evidence: the digest models a later claim from an acquaintance and scores a source-aware answer as correct, whereas Loftus and Palmer manipulated the wording of the experimenter's question and did not test source tags or source-aware corrections.

## Scope limits the digest does not carry (population, method, delay, effect size)

- Undergraduate students in a laboratory film-viewing task; the event was a short traffic-accident film, not a lived episode in a long stream.
- The manipulation is question wording by the experimenter ("smashed" versus "hit" and other verbs), not a separate social source making a false claim, and not a retraction or reliability manipulation.
- The one-week retest concerns a single detail (broken glass) after one post-event questionnaire; the digest's FM2 spans 7-90 days and 1 or 3 misleading exposures.
- The authors discuss both response bias and a changed memory representation; the study does not establish source monitoring failure or that the original trace is erased.
- The paper does not test the digest's scoring rule for a source-aware answer ("blue, though someone later said red"), so that rule is an extension.
- Effect sizes are condition-specific percentages (for broken glass, .32 versus .14); there is no single general misinformation effect size.
